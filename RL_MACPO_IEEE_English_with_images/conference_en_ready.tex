\title{RL-MACPO: An Adaptive Communication Architecture with Conflict Gating and Learned Penalty Control for Network-Based Distributed Optimization}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Network-based distributed optimization in multi-agent systems often wastes effort on unnecessary negotiation because penalty-based cooperative frameworks such as MACPO open communication on nearly every outer loop. This paper proposes RL-MACPO, an \emph{adaptive communication architecture} on the MACPO backbone that combines conflict-gated communication, variable selection within the negotiation branch, and adaptive penalty control implemented by a lightweight reinforcement-learning (RL) policy. The design goal is to trigger communication only when subsequent negotiation is likely to improve shared-variable consistency, rather than to replace MACPO with an RL optimizer. Experiments on the MACPO NDO benchmark, MACPO-style dispatch simulators, and IEEE transmission-network pilots show that RL-MACPO substantially reduces communication while preserving or improving final fitness under identical evaluation budgets. Mechanism and ablation studies further indicate that the coordinated gating and selection architecture---not the choice of penalty controller alone---accounts for the main gains.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
network-based distributed optimization, black-box optimization, multi-agent cooperative optimization, adaptive communication, conflict gating, reinforcement learning
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Network-based distributed optimization (NDO) arises naturally in modern networked systems such as robotics~\cite{ref_dopt_robotics_2024}, smart grids~\cite{ref_dopt_smartgrid_2024}, wireless sensor networks~\cite{ref_dopt_wsn_2023}, and the Internet of Vehicles~\cite{ref_dopt_iov_2024}, where decision making is distributed over many interconnected nodes rather than handled by a single central controller~\cite{ref_decentralized_review_2023}. In these systems, each node usually has access only to local information, while system-level performance depends on the coordination of multiple agents over a communication network. This makes distributed optimization a natural modeling and algorithmic framework for many real applications.

A typical feature of NDO is that the decision variables are not purely local. In many practical scenarios, the optimization problem contains both private variables owned by individual agents and shared variables that directly couple neighboring agents. This mixed-variable structure appears in transportation flow assignment~\cite{ref_ndo_traffic_2022}, electric dispatching~\cite{ref_ndo_dispatch_2024}, distributed state estimation~\cite{ref_ndo_stateest_2024}, and related networked control problems~\cite{ref_ndo_netcontrol_2024}. When objectives are black-box and only locally observable, the central difficulty becomes efficient coordination on shared variables under limited communication (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Penalty-based cooperative evolution---exemplified by MACPO \cite{ref_macpo}---provides a practical NDO backbone, yet still triggers negotiation on nearly every outer loop and relies on static or manually tuned penalty control.

Although these methods have advanced the field, three limitations remain common in distributed black-box optimization. First, penalty and coordination parameters are often static or manually tuned, which limits adaptivity across conflict patterns and search stages. Second, communication and negotiation can dominate runtime in large or conflict-heterogeneous instances when negotiation is triggered on nearly every outer loop. Third, the cost of negotiation scales with both the number of shared variables processed and the frequency of communication, so always-on coordination becomes increasingly expensive as problem scale grows.

Reinforcement learning is a sequential decision framework in which an agent observes the current state, takes an action, and updates its policy from reward feedback. In RL-MACPO it is used only inside the penalty-control module: it does not replace MACPO's evolutionary search or the rule-based communication gate.

The central problem addressed in this paper is \textbf{how to reduce unnecessary communication in network-based distributed optimization while preserving optimization quality}. MACPO opens communication and negotiates on nearly every outer loop; when conflict is weak or stale, this coordination cost may not translate into better shared-variable consistency or final fitness. Our response is an \textbf{adaptive communication architecture}---not a standalone RL penalty algorithm---with one design goal: \emph{communicate only when subsequent negotiation is worth the cost, and negotiate only on the shared dimensions that matter}.

RL-MACPO realizes this architecture through three coordinated modules on the MACPO backbone. \textbf{Conflict-gated communication} decides \emph{whether} to open the negotiation channel from online conflict signals, together with interval, relative-threshold, and fail-safe rules. \textbf{Variable selection for negotiation} decides \emph{which} shared dimensions enter negotiation once communication is granted. \textbf{Adaptive penalty control} adjusts penalty strength from conflict dynamics through a common interface that supports RL and heuristic controllers; RL serves as a unified default controller without benchmark-specific schedule design. Fig.~\ref{fig:rl_macpo_architecture} summarizes the framework.

The contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An adaptive communication architecture} for MACPO-style distributed black-box optimization, combining conflict-gated communication, variable selection inside the negotiation branch, and adaptive penalty control as one coordinated design (Section~\ref{sec:method}, Algorithms~\ref{alg:ci_gating}--\ref{alg:negotiation_consistency} and~\ref{alg:rl_penalty_update}).
\item \textbf{An adaptive penalty-control interface} that supports RL and heuristic controllers under the same gating and selection architecture, with RL as a unified default controller that avoids benchmark-specific penalty schedules (Section~\ref{sec:penalty_interface}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl_penalty_update}).
\item \textbf{Comprehensive validation} on the MACPO NDO benchmark, mechanism and ablation studies, and MACPO-style dispatch simulators plus IEEE transmission-network pilots, organized around communication efficiency, controller choice, and module contributions (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

The paper first introduces the problem formulation and the original MACPO framework, then presents RL-MACPO, followed by experimental evaluation organized around four questions, complexity analysis, discussion, and conclusions.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
NDO has been studied in traffic assignment, power dispatch, distributed state estimation, and other networked systems, where each agent owns local information while shared variables couple neighboring links. Recent surveys of decentralized optimization emphasize information flow, time-varying topology, and communication cost in decomposition-based methods \cite{ref_decentralized_review_2023,ref_privacy_dist_opt_2025}.

Gradient-based distributed optimization---including DGD, dual averaging, ADMM, EXTRA, and Push--Pull---provides strong convergence guarantees when objectives are convex and gradients are available \cite{ref_nedic_subgrad,ref_duchi_dualavg,ref_boyd_admm,ref_shi_extra,ref_pu_pushpull_2021,ref_koloskova_decsgd_2020}. Federated learning and Local SGD similarly pursue communication-efficient coordination, though typically for differentiable models rather than explicit shared-variable negotiation on general NDO graphs \cite{ref_mcmahan_fedavg_2017,ref_stich_local_sgd_2019,ref_kairouz_flsurvey_2021}. These approaches are less directly applicable when the objective is black-box, highly nonconvex, or accessible only through simulation \cite{ref_back_book,ref_hansen_es,ref_storn_de_1997,ref_kennedy_pso_1995,ref_blackbox_tracking_2022}.

Within gradient-free optimization, penalty methods and cooperative coevolution provide a practical path for large-scale black-box problems \cite{ref_deb_constraint,ref_coello_survey,ref_potter_dejong,ref_omidvar_grouping,ref_dg2,ref_cec2010,ref_cec2013,ref_highdim_survey_2024,ref_li_yao_survey}. Ding et al.\ \cite{ref_cc_dist_accel_2024} recently analyzed distributed accelerations of cooperative coevolution on non-separable instances; such work improves scalable \emph{global} black-box search but does not address the mixed private/shared-variable topology and neighbor negotiation structure central to NDO.

For networked distributed \emph{black-box} optimization specifically, several recent methods pursue adaptive coordination without analytic gradients. MACPO \cite{ref_macpo} combines penalty-based local objectives with conflict-aware neighbor negotiation on shared-edge variables, but opens communication on nearly every outer loop with static penalty control. MASOIE \cite{ref_masoie_2024} uses internal/external swarm learning for consensus-based distributed optimization and adapts the communication interval across search stages. MAES-CCSA \cite{ref_maes_ccsa_2025} targets black-box distributed optimization with cooperative cumulative step-size adaptation driven by multi-agent evolution-path consistency. These methods differ in problem structure (NDO penalty negotiation vs.\ consensus swarms vs.\ DBO sampling), yet share the goal of reducing redundant coordination while preserving solution quality. None jointly implements conflict-gated \emph{when}-to-communicate, dimension-level \emph{what}-to-negotiate, and a plug-in penalty interface under one architecture---the gap addressed by RL-MACPO.

Reinforcement learning has been applied to metaheuristic parameter control, operator selection, and online adaptation \cite{ref_karafotias_param_control,ref_rl_metaheuristic_2023,ref_rl_ea_survey_2024,ref_andrychowicz_l2l,ref_schulman_ppo_2017,ref_haarnoja_sac_2018}. In distributed black-box settings with explicit shared-variable negotiation, RL has mostly appeared as a penalty or step-size tuner rather than as one module inside a broader communication architecture. RL-MACPO uses RL in the latter role: the primary novelty is when and what to negotiate, not a claim that RL penalties universally dominate hand-crafted schedules.

\section{Problem Definition}\label{sec:problem}
Consider $n$ agents on an undirected graph $G=(V,E)$ with global decision vector $X=[x_1,\ldots,x_D]$. For agent $i$,
\begin{equation}
X_i = \mathrm{col}\{x_d\}_{d \in \Theta_i}, \quad \Theta_i = I_i \cup \bigcup_{j \in N_i} I_{i,j}
\label{eq:local_variable_set}
\end{equation}
where $I_i \subseteq \{1,2,\ldots,D\}$ is the index set of private variables on vertex $i$, $I_{i,j}$ is the index set of shared variables on edge $(i,j)$, and $N_i$ is the neighbor set of agent $i$. The global objective is
\begin{equation}
\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^D} F(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(X_i)
\label{eq:global_objective}
\end{equation}
where $f_i$ is the local objective of agent $i$. Under the distributed setting, this problem is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\min_{X_1,\ldots,X_n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(X_i)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_i^d = x_j^d,\ \forall d\in I_{i,j},\ \forall (i,j)\in E.
\label{eq:distributed_formulation}
\end{equation}

\section{Original MACPO Framework}\label{sec:macpo_prelim}
\noindent
The original Multi-Agent Cooperative Penalty Optimization (MACPO) framework balances local optimization and cross-agent consistency through a penalty-based local objective and a negotiation stage between neighbors \cite{ref_macpo}. For agent $i$, the local objective is written as
\begin{equation}
h_i(x)=f_i(x)+\alpha_i c_i(x)
\label{eq:macpo_local_objective}
\end{equation}
where $f_i(x)$ is the local objective, $c_i(x)$ measures the deviation from the neighborhood consensus solution, and $\alpha_i$ is the penalty weight.

Each iteration alternates local population search with neighbor negotiation on shared variables. RL-MACPO keeps this backbone and modifies penalty adaptation, communication activation, and negotiation scope.

\section{Proposed RL-MACPO}\label{sec:method}
RL-MACPO is an adaptive communication architecture on the MACPO backbone (Section~\ref{sec:macpo_prelim}). Throughout the paper, \emph{communication} means the gate decision (whether to open the channel) and \emph{negotiation} means shared-variable processing after communication is granted. The default \emph{Full} configuration enables conflict gating, variable selection, and adaptive penalty control with RL as the default implementation; ablations disable individual modules. Fig.~\ref{fig:rl_macpo_architecture}(a) shows the per-iteration pipeline; Fig.~\ref{fig:rl_macpo_architecture}(b) details the penalty-control loop after re-evaluation.

\subsection{Gating Communication with the Conflict Index}
Conflict gating decides \emph{whether} to open communication before paying the cost of neighbor negotiation. Intuitively, communication is triggered when the expected consistency benefit outweighs its cost; $\mathrm{CI}_i^t$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_gate} acts as a black-box proxy for that benefit by measuring how far local shared values deviate from the neighborhood reference. A three-level gate (minimum interval, relative threshold with fail-safe, phase sampling) skips low-benefit rounds while still opening the channel when conflict rises.
\begin{equation}
g_i^t=\mathbf{1}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}^t\land \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{thr}}^t\land \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phase}}^t\right]
\label{eq:local_gate}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}^t &: \text{minimum communication interval is satisfied},\\
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{thr}}^t &: \mathrm{CI}_i^t > \lambda\,\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CI},i}^t\ \text{or}\ t-t_{\mathrm{last\_comm}}\ge K,\\
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phase}}^t &: u_i^t < p_{\mathrm{phase}}^t,\quad u_i^t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1).
\end{align}
Here $\mathrm{CI}_i^t$ is a robust local conflict proxy computed from the difference between the local solution and the reference solution, $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CI},i}^t$ characterizes the typical scale of the conflict signal in the current phase, $\lambda$ controls the relative threshold strength, $K$ controls the longest silent interval, and $p_{\mathrm{phase}}^t$ adjusts communication frequency across search stages.

A crucial point is that $\mathrm{CI}_i^t$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_gate} is a black-box conflict proxy motivated by the gradient-based conflict index in \cite{ref_macpo}, not a direct gradient estimate. For each overlapping dimension $d$, the proxy aggregates normalized discrepancies between the local best and the consensus reference; $\mathrm{CI}_i^t$ is their mean over the active overlap set and is compared to the EMA baseline $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CI},i}^t$.

\paragraph{Design rationale.} Layer~1 (minimum interval) prevents back-to-back negotiations that add cost without fresh conflict information. Layer~2 (relative threshold with fail-safe $K$) opens communication only when local conflict exceeds its recent baseline, filtering weak or stale inconsistency; the fail-safe guarantees progress when conflict stays flat. Layer~3 (phase sampling) modulates communication density across search stages without per-function schedules. Together, these layers implement selective communication---the primary lever for the substantial communication reductions reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp_q2}.

In the parallel implementation, the final communication decision is obtained by global reduction:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{global}}^t=\max_i g_i^t
\label{eq:global_gate}
\end{equation}
If any agent triggers communication, the relevant neighborhood enters the negotiation branch. Otherwise the algorithm follows the skip-negotiation branch and only refreshes the consensus reference.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ci_gating} summarizes the gating procedure. Gate variants (always-on, fixed absolute threshold, relative threshold, relative threshold with fail-safe) are evaluated empirically in Section~\ref{sec:gate_validation}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Conflict-Gated Communication with Phase Sampling (Abstract)}
  \label{alg:ci_gating}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require Local-best $x_i^\star$, consensus reference $x_{i,\mathrm{con}}$, overlap set $\Theta_i$, communication records
  \Ensure Global gate $g_{\mathrm{global}}^t$, communication branch selection
  \For{each optimization iteration $t$}
    \State Compute local conflict index $\mathrm{CI}_i^t$ on $\Theta_i$
    \State Update EMA statistic $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CI},i}^t$ and the last-communication record
    \State Check interval constraint $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}^t$ from communication cooldown
    \State Check threshold constraint $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{thr}}^t: \mathrm{CI}_i^t > \lambda\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CI},i}^t\ \text{or}\ t-t_{\mathrm{last\_comm}}\ge K$
    \State Draw phase sample $u_i^t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$
    \State Check phase constraint $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phase}}^t: u_i^t<p_{\mathrm{phase}}^t$
    \State Local gate:
    \Statex \hspace{1.5em}$g_i^t\gets \mathbf{1}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}^t\land \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{thr}}^t\land \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phase}}^t\right]$
    \State Global reduction $g_{\mathrm{global}}^t\gets \max_i g_i^t$
    \If{$g_{\mathrm{global}}^t=0$}
      \State Follow skip-negotiation branch and keep local evolution only
      \State Refresh consensus reference from local-best maintenance rule
    \Else
      \State Enter negotiation branch for active neighbor pairs
      \State Execute exchange, competition, consistency check, and sharing in sequence
    \EndIf
    \State Update communication counters, interval timers, and threshold state
  \EndFor
  \State \Return Communication decisions and branch traces
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This can be viewed as importance sampling over shared variables---negotiation effort concentrates on dimensions whose historical conflict contribution is largest. The \emph{NoSelection} ablation disables this filter.

The goal of variable selection is to prioritize high-impact dimensions. For each shared variable $d$, the importance score evolves as
\begin{equation}
q_d^{t+1}=\gamma q_d^t+(1-\gamma)\,\xi_d^t,
\end{equation}
where $\xi_d^t$ combines the current conflict contribution and the historical effectiveness of negotiation, and $\gamma\in(0,1)$ is a smoothing factor.

On the candidate dimension set $\Theta_i$, the dimensions are ranked by $q_d^t$ and a subset $\Omega_i^t\subseteq\Theta_i$ is selected for negotiation.

\paragraph{Role in communication quality.} Skipping low-$q_d$ dimensions avoids negotiation perturbations on shared variables that are already near consensus. Combined with gating, this shifts coordination from quantity (how often agents communicate) to quality (which rounds and dimensions are worth updating). Section~\ref{sec:q3_modules} ablates gating depth and related knobs; selection is part of the default \emph{Full} stack throughout the main benchmark.

\subsection{Conflict Detection and Solution Exchange}
When communication is active, neighbors exchange local-best candidates and apply Nash-style bilateral comparison followed by two-sided perturbation checking (Algorithm~\ref{alg:negotiation_consistency}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Negotiation with Two-Sided Consistency Check (Abstract)}
  \label{alg:negotiation_consistency}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require Neighbor pair $(i,j)$, local-best candidates $x_i^\star,x_j^\star$, shared set $L_{i,j}$, variable switch $\mathrm{switch}[\cdot]$
  \Ensure Updated shared variables, consensus reference, and negotiation-success flag
  \State Exchange $x_i^\star$ and $x_j^\star$ between neighbors
  \State Initialize negotiation-success flag as false
  \For{each shared dimension $d\in L_{i,j}$}
    \If{$\mathrm{switch}[d]=0$}
      \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \State Build local trial and cross trial for dimension $d$
    \State Evaluate utilities $u_{\mathrm{self}}(d)$, $u_{\mathrm{cross}}(d)$,
    \Statex \hspace{1.5em}$u_{\mathrm{cross,self}}(d)$, $u_{\mathrm{self,cross}}(d)$
    \If{$u_{\mathrm{self}}(d)+u_{\mathrm{cross,self}}(d) > u_{\mathrm{cross}}(d)+u_{\mathrm{self,cross}}(d)$}
      \State Select neighbor value for $d$
    \Else
      \State Keep local value for $d$
    \EndIf
    \State Perform two-sided perturbation around selected value on $d$
    \State Obtain responses $(\delta_i^+,\delta_i^-)$ and $(\delta_j^+,\delta_j^-)$
    \If{$(\delta_i^+\delta_j^+>0)\land(\delta_i^-\delta_j^->0)$}
      \State $\mathrm{switch}[d]\gets 0$
    \Else
      \State Keep $\mathrm{switch}[d]$ active for future renegotiation
    \EndIf
    \State If accepted by sharing rule, write updated $d$ into consensus reference
    \State Update negotiation-success flag when at least one dimension changes
  \EndFor
  \State Re-evaluate local population under new consensus reference
  \State \Return Consensus reference, $\mathrm{switch}[\cdot]$, negotiation-success flag
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Adaptive Penalty Control Interface}\label{sec:penalty_interface}
The penalty module exposes a common interface---conflict signals in, bounded penalty ratio out---implemented by RL (default), EMA smoothing, or fixed phase schedules (Table~\ref{tab:penalty_controller_f3_f5}). All implementations share the same ratio mapping; they differ only in how $\rho_i^{t+1}$ is updated. RL provides a unified default controller without benchmark-specific schedule design while remaining statistically tied with heuristics under fixed gating---not because penalty choice dominates the overall gain.

The conflict term is
\begin{equation}
c_i(x)=\sum_{d\in\Theta_i}\frac{|x^d-x_{i,\mathrm{con}}^d|}{u_d-l_d}
\label{eq:conflict_term}
\end{equation}
State, action, and ratio mapping follow
\begin{equation}
s_i^t=[c_i^t,\ \Delta c_i^t],\qquad \Delta c_i^t=c_i^t-c_i^{t-1}
\label{eq:rl_state}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a_i^t=\pi_\theta(s_i^t),\qquad a_i^t\in(-1,1)
\label{eq:rl_action}
\end{equation}
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\rho_i^{t+1}&=\Pi_{[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]}
\left(\rho_i^t+\eta_a a_i^t\right),
\label{eq:rho_update}\\
\alpha_i^{t+1}&=\bar{\alpha}_i^{t+1}\rho_i^{t+1}.
\label{eq:alpha_mapping}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here $\Pi$ is interval projection, $\bar{\alpha}_i$ is a baseline penalty related to objective scale, and $\rho_i$ provides online adaptation. Under the RL default, reward couples conflict reduction and pure-objective improvement through a bounded odd map $\psi(\cdot)$:
\begin{equation}
r_i^t=\psi\left(-\Delta c_{i,\mathrm{rate}}^t\cdot \Delta f_{i,\mathrm{pure,rate}}^t\right)
\label{eq:rl_reward}
\end{equation}
EMA and fixed schedules plug into the same $\rho$--$\alpha$ mapping without policy learning. Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl_penalty_update} summarizes the RL default; heuristic controllers replace the policy/replay steps with their own $\rho$-update rules.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Adaptive Penalty Control with RL Default (Abstract)}
  \label{alg:rl_penalty_update}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require Conflict and baseline-penalty streams, policy $\pi_{\theta_i}$, replay buffer $\mathcal{B}_i$
  \Ensure Updated $\{\rho_i^t,\alpha_i^t,\theta_i\}$
  \For{each optimization iteration $t$}
    \State Form $s_i^t=[c_i^t,\Delta c_i^t]$ from shared-variable conflict (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:conflict_term}--\eqref{eq:rl_state})
    \State Infer $a_i^t=\pi_{\theta_i}(s_i^t)$ and map to $\rho_i^{t+1},\alpha_i^{t+1}$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rho_update}--\eqref{eq:alpha_mapping})
    \State Optimize locally with $h_i(x)=f_i(x)+\alpha_i^{t+1}c_i(x)$; compute $r_i^t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rl_reward})
    \State Append $(s_i^t,a_i^t,r_i^t)$ to $\mathcal{B}_i$ and update $\theta_i$ by prioritized replay when triggered
  \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Which architecture modules matter?}\label{sec:q3_modules}

\paragraph{Conflict gating.} Without Layer~2 (relative threshold), gating collapses variance on F1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:gating_ablation}). No-gating and Layer1-only means exceed MACPO with coefficient of variation up to 17.6\%. Layer1+2 and Full restore the Q1 mean (about 6.3 million) with low variance. Layer~2 is necessary. \paragraph{Q4 analysis.} On dispatch simulators, RL-MACPO substantially lowers communication while changing best $f_{\mathrm{pure}}$ by at most 1.8\% (Table~\ref{tab:application_cases}). Resource scheduling improves by about 1.8\%. The same gate-and-penalty stack transfers because these cases share MACPO's shared/private variable structure. \section{Complexity Analysis}\label{sec:complexity}
Under always-on MACPO negotiation, communication and negotiation scale linearly with outer iterations $I$, edges $|E|$, and shared dimensions $|L_{i,j}|$. RL-MACPO replaces fixed per-round negotiation by average trigger rate $\bar p_{\mathrm{comm}}$ and active-dimension ratio $\bar r_{\mathrm{act}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{nego}}^{\mathrm{RL\mbox{-}MACPO}}\right]
=
\mathcal{O}\!\left(
I\,\bar p_{\mathrm{comm}}\,\bar r_{\mathrm{act}}
\sum_{(i,j)\in E}|L_{i,j}|\,C_f
\right)
+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RL}}),
\label{eq:nego_complexity}
\end{equation}
with expected communication $\mathcal{O}(I\,\bar p_{\mathrm{comm}}\,|E|)$. The large communication reductions reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp_q2} lower $\bar p_{\mathrm{comm}}$. Variable selection lowers $\bar r_{\mathrm{act}}$. Gating variants are validated empirically in Section~\ref{sec:gate_validation}. Extended periodic-baseline tables are omitted for space.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Mechanistic insight.} MACPO's always-on negotiation repeatedly perturbs shared variables even when local conflict is weak. Conflict gating suppresses low-benefit rounds (Eq.~\eqref{eq:local_gate}), and variable selection limits negotiation to high-impact dimensions, shifting coordination from quantity to quality. On F1--F18, communication becomes much sparser while fitness improves at the same budget (Tables~\ref{tab:macpo_style_all},~\ref{tab:f1f6_comm_eva}). Table~\ref{tab:penalty_controller_f3_f5} adds that, once gating and selection are fixed, penalty-controller choice is not dominant. Several adaptive implementations are statistically tied, so the architecture rather than a particular penalty schedule accounts for most of the gain.

\paragraph{Practical implication.} RL is retained as the default penalty implementation because it provides a unified adaptive controller without benchmark-specific schedule design while matching EMA and fixed schedules on F3/F5 without significant Wilcoxon differences. Fixed schedules require hand-designed phase or interval rules. EMA requires smoothing parameters and update heuristics. RL learns the adjustment policy from optimization feedback (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rl_reward}) through the shared penalty interface. That is an automation advantage, not a claim of universal superiority.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Comparisons are mainly against MACPO and internal variants rather than a broad set of external distributed black-box optimizers. Gate validation (Table~\ref{tab:gate_cost_stats}) uses a 30-run F1/F2/F5 subset. IEEE~118 shows a small detectable cost offset alongside much lower communication. IEEE~30 has two seed-level stagnation outliers unrelated to communication rate. We do not provide convergence guarantees for the RL-augmented loop. Asynchronous graphs and multi-hop negotiation remain open extensions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
RL-MACPO proposes an adaptive communication architecture for network-based distributed black-box optimization. It combines conflict-gated communication, variable selection, and an adaptive penalty-control interface on the MACPO backbone. On F1--F18 it improves final fitness at the same evaluation budget while opening communication on only a small fraction of outer loops. Dispatch pilots show similar savings with preserved objective quality. Controller comparisons on F3/F5 confirm that the architecture rather than penalty choice alone accounts for most of the gain. RL serves as the unified default controller without benchmark-specific schedule design.
